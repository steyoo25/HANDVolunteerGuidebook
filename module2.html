The Foundation

The Importance of Music for Cognitive Health

For seniors, music is often far more than simply sound or entertainment. It can function as therapy to reduce anxiety, manage agitation, and build social connection. Studies have shown improvements in mood, memory, sleep, and physical recovery through consistent music therapy sessions.

What Is Music Therapy?

Music Therapy is the practice of using music interventions to achieve individualized goals. This can involve singing, composing, or listening to music.

For individuals with dementia or Alzheimer's disease, personalized playlists of classics from their youth can trigger memory recall, foster communication, and enhance emotional responses—even in individuals who normally don't express themselves well.

Benefits of Music for Seniors

Cognitive Benefits

  • Slows cognitive decline
  • Enhances memory
  • Stimulates mental activity

Emotional Upliftment

  • Reduces stress and anxiety
  • Boosts mood
  • Provides emotional outlet

Social Connection

  • Group music activities
  • Combats loneliness
  • Fosters intergenerational bonds

Physical Health

  • Encourages movement
  • Supports motor skills
  • Manages pain
🧠
Research Finding

75% of seniors report that music relieves stress or relaxes them. 73% say it brings them joy. Music therapy is one of the most evidence-supported non-medication interventions for dementia.

Building Connection

Connecting With Residents Through Music

Many seniors hold strong emotional connections to the music they grew up with. Playing familiar songs can reawaken dormant memories, spark meaningful conversations, and elicit joy in their day.

How to Start Musical Conversations

Ask about their preferences: Some residents may prefer specific genres like jazz, country, gospel, or classical. Others have favorite artists or songs. If residents are unsure, try songs from the 1950s–1980s, which tend to resonate most with seniors.

Share concert memories: Ask residents about concerts they've attended in the past and how that experience made them feel. You can also ask about musicians or concerts they wish they'd seen in person.

Through simple questions and shared listening experiences, music can become a meaningful way to build connection, stimulate memory, and foster enjoyable conversations.

Group Activities

Group Music and Team Building

One of the most beautiful and powerful components of music is its ability to be shared and to cultivate joyful emotions and memories for groups of people.

When Multiple Volunteers Play Together

When two or more volunteers play instruments or sing collaboratively, the music experience becomes more engaging for residents. Simple harmonies or call-and-response patterns can create a sense of participation and shared joy while boosting confidence for both volunteers and residents. Playing music outside with residents can also boost mood and create positive connections with sound and nature.

Beyond Live Music

Group music doesn't always require live performance! Consider:

  • Karaoke nights and group playlists: Fun ways to involve residents and foster team connection
  • Musical games: Music bingo or trivia encourage residents to listen, remember, and celebrate together
  • Movement-based activities: "Just Dance" sessions or drum circles allow residents to translate music into bodily expression, promoting teamwork, cognitive stimulation, and joy. Light exercise through music is always beneficial.
✨
Tip for Success

Coordinate with staff at each site to ensure smooth transitions, favorite song selections, and inclusive participation. The more you collaborate with the care team, the better the experience for residents.

Individual Engagement

One-on-One Music Engagement

Spending time one-on-one with older adults living with dementia can be deeply meaningful. Music is a powerful way to connect even when words are hard to find.

Getting Started

Start by playing a familiar song or humming a tune and watch how the resident responds. If you notice them smile, tap their foot, or begin to sing, follow their lead and encourage them. If they seem unsure, be patient, slow down, and try a different song.

Gently say things like: "Would you like to clap with me?" or "You can join in however you'd like." This offers a space of creative engagement without pressure.

Song Choice Matters

Rather than loud or overwhelming sounds, choose soothing music and rhythms that are meaningful to them. The goal is not to be a musician, but to have fun with residents and encourage their participation.

Create Music Together

A great way to engage is offering them the opportunity to create music with you. Offer simple instruments like maracas, bongos, and tambourines. Invite them to clap and play along to the beat. Celebrate even the small moments to make residents feel excited and seen.

💭
Remember

Music is more than entertainment—it can meaningfully make all the difference in residents' mental and emotional well-being. These moments foster connection and belonging through shared musical experiences and undivided interaction. Even after your visit ends, the impact can continue. Residents may carry the feeling of the song with them and find small moments of joy throughout their day.

Growing the Program

Future Ideas

Beyond incorporating music into engagement sessions, volunteers are encouraged to bring suggestions for music initiatives to the HAND executive team.

Moving Ideas to Programs

Having tangible goalposts can turn new ideas into sustainable programming, ensuring that music and sound-based engagement becomes a consistent, intentional part of residents' experiences.

For example, you might want to:

  • Offer a group music event with percussion instruments for residents
  • Build a long-term instrument closet so future volunteers can run similar sessions without starting from scratch
  • Coordinate a spring fundraiser to build sustainable music programming

However, moving from an idea to a durable program requires coordination across several parts of HAND. Volunteers should articulate the purpose of the suggested initiative and work with the executive team to ensure that the project aligns with HAND's broader goals. The executive team can then help coordinate fundraising and direct volunteers toward campus groups or partner organizations that can offer donations.
