Foundations

What You Need to Know

As you work with individuals with neurological disorders, learning about these conditions will help you better interact with and support the people you encounter. In this module, we focus on dementia—the most prevalent condition Duke HAND volunteers encounter.

What Are Neurological Disorders?

Neurological disorders describe a large variety of conditions that affect the brain, spinal cord, or nervous system. Some of the most common conditions are strokes, headaches, and dementia. These are typically serious conditions that can impact memory, movement, behavior, and cognition.

What Is Dementia?

People sometimes confuse "dementia" and "Alzheimer's disease," but they're not the same:

  • Dementia is an umbrella term describing brain disease that affects memory, thinking, and normal functioning.
  • Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia (about 60–80% of cases). It's typically characterized by amyloid plaques or tau tangles in the brain.

While it's not necessary to know the specific cause of dementia your patient has, we encourage you to explore other causes if you have time!

Why Is Dementia Important?

Dementia is a global health crisis. According to the World Health Organization, dementia is the seventh largest cause of death globally, affecting millions of people worldwide. It impacts not only individuals but also their families and support systems. Understanding dementia helps caregivers provide compassionate, informed support during this difficult time of life.

Neuroscience

The Healthy Brain

Before understanding what goes wrong in dementia, it helps to have a basic picture of how a healthy brain works.

Three Key Brain Regions

Three brain regions are especially important when thinking about dementia:

🧠

Hippocampus

Forms and processes new memories

🔍

Cortex

Thinking, decision-making, and language

💛

Amygdala

Processes emotions and emotional responses

As you'll see, dementia can affect all three of these areas, which is why the condition impacts so much of a person's life.

How Brain Cells Communicate

Your brain is made up of billions of neurons that constantly communicate with each other through chemical signals sent across synapses. These signals are responsible for just about everything: your mood, your ability to learn, and your memory. When this communication system works well, everything runs smoothly. But when something disrupts it, the effects can be devastating.

Normal Aging vs. Dementia

Not all memory issues mean dementia—this is important to understand as a volunteer. It's somewhat normal to experience forgetfulness with age. However, the key difference is that dementia memory loss becomes severe enough to interfere with everyday life.

✓ Normal Aging ⚠ Dementia Occasional forgetfulness Forgetting close family members Slower processing speed Getting lost in familiar places Needing extra time to recall names Unable to perform basic daily tasks Misplacing items occasionally Confusion that disrupts daily life

This distinction will help you approach your patients with increased understanding and empathy.

Most Common Cause

Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia. It is a progressive brain disorder, meaning symptoms gradually worsen over time.

What Happens in the Brain?

In Alzheimer's, two abnormal proteins build up in the brain and damage neurons:

  • Amyloid plaques: Clumps that form between brain cells and disrupt communication.
  • Tau tangles: Twisted fibers that build up inside cells and interfere with their ability to function.

Together, these changes cause brain cells to lose their connections to one another and eventually deteriorate. Over time, mass cell death causes parts of the brain to physically shrink—particularly the hippocampus, which is why memory loss is often one of the earliest symptoms.

The Three Stages of Alzheimer's

Alzheimer's is typically described in three stages. Knowing these will help you understand where your patient is in their journey and how to interact with them effectively.

Early Stage
  • Memory loss
  • Frequently misplacing items
  • Difficulty finding words
Middle Stage
  • Increased confusion
  • Personality or mood changes
  • Difficulty with everyday tasks
Late Stage
  • Severe memory loss
  • Difficulty communicating
  • Significant physical decline
Clinical Care

Diagnosis & Monitoring

How Is Dementia Diagnosed?

Diagnosing dementia involves several steps:

  • A doctor reviews the patient's medical history and discusses symptoms
  • Cognitive tests assess memory, thinking, and problem-solving abilities
  • Brain imaging may be used to rule out other potential causes

Why Early Diagnosis Matters

Care & Hope

Treatment & Support

Current Treatments

While there is currently no cure for dementia, patients have options and hope. Medications are available that can help manage symptoms, and newer treatments are being developed that aim to slow disease progression in some patients. Research is very active and the landscape of available treatments continues to evolve.

Non-Medication Approaches

Beyond medication, several non-medical approaches have been shown to meaningfully improve quality of life for dementia patients. Many of your interactions as a volunteer directly support these approaches, making your role more impactful than you might realize!

🧩

Mental Stimulation

Puzzles, reading, and conversation keep the mind active

🏃

Physical Activity

Regular exercise supports brain and body health

🤝

Social Engagement

You play a huge role in combating isolation

📅

Structured Routines

Familiarity and predictability bring comfort and calm

Your Role as a Volunteer

Each time you engage with a patient—whether through conversation, a game, or simply being present—you are directly contributing to their mental stimulation and social engagement. These interactions have real and measurable benefits for their quality of life. Thank you for the incredible work you do!
