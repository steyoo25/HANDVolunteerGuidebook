Timeslips Creative Engagement

Learn the Timeslips method for imagination-focused storytelling with dementia patients.

The Mindset

Philosophy of Creative Engagement

Goal: Shift the focus from "what is true" to "what is possible." We want to relieve the pressure of memory.

What Is Creativity?

In this context, creativity doesn't mean coming up with something original or novel. Creativity is simply the action of expressing something, emphasizing imagination over accuracy. Move away from the idea of "testing" the seniors and think of it more as building something together with them.

Why Engagement Matters

Engagement tries to counter the feeling of isolation that often comes with memory loss. When we engage creatively, we give people back their agency and a sense of purpose. This is profound work.

A Brief Note About Dementia

While dementia often impacts short-term memory and linear thinking, the capacity for imagination and emotional resonance remains incredibly strong. With Timeslips, we are tapping into what is still there in their imagination.

💭
Key Insight

"Creativity is adding something new to the world that has value." – Gene Kramer. In Timeslips, we honor the creative potential that remains, no matter the stage of cognitive decline.

Foundational Ideas

Core Principles of Timeslips

An Open Mindset

An open mindset is core to creative engagement activities. It's important to remember to focus on and encourage people's imaginations, and that there are no wrong answers. A good way to remember this is "yes, and" thinking.

This type of thinking emphasizes opening yourself to wonder, validating people's feelings and their meaning.

Facilitation Techniques

Examples of good ways to facilitate creative storytelling are:

  • Asking beautiful questions: Open-ended inquiries to spark creativity and thinking
  • Echoing people's responses: Demonstrating that you're actively listening to them

Individual vs. Group Storytelling

Both individual and group stories work; you can choose which type depending on what you're looking for and what others would prefer.

👤

Individual Stories

Perfect if the person prefers more control over the session and enjoys using their imagination to create a deeper story. May not work well if the person is hesitant or disengaged.

👥

Group Stories

Works well because it helps reinforce a sense of community among participants and is often more playful and funny—as long as the group can collaborate effectively.

Practical Guide

Running a Creative Engagement Session

What You Need to Facilitate a Session

Keep it minimal:

  • A compelling, slightly ambiguous prompt (like a quirky photograph or interesting object)
  • A quiet and comfortable space
  • An open mind
  • Something to write their ideas down on (so they see their words have value)

Specific Steps

  1. 1
    Introduce yourself and pass around the image/object. You could add: "There are no wrong answers."
  2. 2
    Ask a broad opening question to get the ball rolling. This should be open-ended and inviting.
  3. 3
    Ask follow-up questions based on what they say. If possible, record their answers visible to the group.
  4. 4
    Read the imaginative story back to them. Celebrate what you built together.

Tips for Communication

✅
The "Yes, and..." Rule

Accept absolutely every answer. If someone says the dog in the picture is flying, try to roll with it: "A flying dog! Where is it flying to?"

  • Give them plenty of time to process the question and answer.
  • Sit at eye level, maintain warm eye contact, and project your voice clearly without shouting.
Question Types

Beautiful Questions

Beautiful questions encourage people's imaginations and promote creativity in storytelling, rather than testing someone's memory or knowledge. They are open-ended, have no right or wrong answers, and invite curiosity and unique expression.

👁 Sensory Questions
  • What do you first notice in this image?
  • What does the air smell like?
  • Is it warm or cold in this place?
  • What sounds are happening here?
📖 Storybuilding Questions
  • Who is this person? What is their name?
  • What's happening here?
  • What happened just before this moment?
  • What might happen next?

Key Techniques to Notice

1

The Open-Ended Name Prompt

"So this man, what do you think his name could be? If you could pick anything."

By not providing a list of names, the volunteer gave total creative freedom. This immediately gave ownership over the story.
2

Building on Non-Visual Details (Yes, And)

"He's a businessman trying to sell newspapers? Definitely. I can see them for sure."

When the resident suggested something imaginative, the volunteer validated it immediately, then pointed to a physical detail in the photo to ground the idea in reality.
3

Sensory Expansion (Music & Mood)

"I'm noticing there's a jukebox right in the background here... what type of music do you think he would enjoy listening to?"

By shifting from what was happening to what was heard, the volunteer opened a new sensory layer that deepened the resident's immersion in the character.
4

Encouraging Narrative Action

"So do you think he gets his ideas maybe from what he sees when he's outside in nature? Is that something that's possible?"

The volunteer used a leading but open question to help connect two different parts of the story, which prompted a specific creative detail to emerge.
🧠
Remember

In real one-on-one sessions, try to be eye-level with the resident. This allows for a more comfortable, personal environment than standing and looking down.
